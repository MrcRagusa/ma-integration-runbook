# Support Channels Setup

## Why this matters disproportionately

In the first 30 days after close, the quality of the support experience for acquired-company users does more to shape their view of the integration than any technical work. Users who feel supported through changes tolerate disruption. Users who feel abandoned during changes resist further integration regardless of how well-planned it is.

This is also where the acquirer's existing service desk often lets the integration down. They've not been briefed, they don't have access to the acquired company's systems, they don't know the acquired company's user population, and they start receiving tickets they can't categorise or resolve. The resulting experience is poor for the user and frustrating for the support team.

Setting up support channels properly before day one prevents most of this.

## Three models

The right support model depends on the size of the acquisition and the maturity of the parent's service desk.

**Embedded support during transition.** The acquired company's existing helpdesk or MSP continues to operate as the primary support channel for acquired users, with the parent's service desk providing back-end support and escalation. Best for medium and large acquisitions where the acquired company has its own internal IT capability.

**Parent service desk with named integration support.** Acquired users go directly to the parent's service desk from day one, but a small named team within the service desk specialises in integration tickets and acquired-company systems. Best for smaller acquisitions where the acquired company has minimal internal IT capability and the support load is manageable.

**Hybrid with phased transition.** Acquired users initially route to a dedicated integration helpdesk, separate from both the parent and the acquired company's normal channels. Over wave 1 and wave 2, they progressively transition to the parent's standard service desk. Best for complex integrations where the user experience needs to be carefully controlled during the transition.

The model decision should be made during pre-close. It affects ticketing platform configuration, staffing, and communication.

## What to set up before day one

**A clear support entry point.** Acquired users need to know exactly where to go for help. A single email address or web link is sufficient. Avoid asking users to remember multiple entry points or to know which one to use for which type of issue.

**Ticket routing logic.** Tickets from acquired-company users need to be identifiable and routable to the right team. The simplest mechanism is to flag tickets coming from acquired-company email domains, but this breaks once domain migration starts. A more durable approach is to tag user records in the ticketing system with their source company and use that tag for routing.

**Knowledge base updates.** The parent's service desk knowledge base needs entries for the acquired company's specific systems, particularly during the period when those systems are still in use. Even if the long-term plan is to retire those systems, the support team needs to know how to handle tickets about them in the meantime.

**Access for support agents.** The integration support team needs administrative or read access to the acquired company's systems sufficient to actually help users. Day-one access provisioning for the support team is one of the technical tasks that needs to happen on day one itself, not later.

**Escalation paths.** Clear escalation routing for the issues the front line can't resolve. During the first 30 days, the acquired company's existing IT lead is usually the right escalation point for system-specific questions; that arrangement needs to be agreed during pre-close, not improvised after close.

## Briefing the parent's existing service desk

The most overlooked part of day-one support setup is briefing the parent's own service desk team. They've not been part of the integration planning, they're seeing the deal announcement at the same time as everyone else, and they're about to start receiving tickets they don't know how to handle.

A 30-minute briefing in the days before close is usually sufficient. Cover: the basic shape of the acquired company, the support model that's been chosen, where to route tickets they can't handle, who the escalation contacts are, and a clear acknowledgment that they're not expected to know the acquired company's systems immediately.

If the integration involves multiple acquired sites or countries, brief the relevant regional support teams separately rather than relying on a single central briefing to cascade.

## Measuring the support experience

I'd recommend tracking three things during the first 30 days, in addition to whatever standard service desk metrics you already use.

**First-contact resolution rate for integration tickets.** A drop in first-contact resolution suggests the support team doesn't yet have the knowledge or access it needs.

**Ticket routing accuracy.** Tickets that get routed wrong on first arrival, then bounced between teams, are a strong indicator of either unclear routing logic or unclear ticket categorisation. Both are fixable.

**User-reported satisfaction on integration tickets.** A simple post-resolution survey, even with a single satisfaction question, generates the early-warning data you need to spot when the support experience is degrading. Anecdotes from senior leadership about specific bad experiences arrive late and are usually unrepresentative.

The point of measuring isn't to score the team; it's to find the operational issues quickly enough to fix them. Most problems in integration support are fixable within a week if they're surfaced early. The same problems left unsurfaced for a month become reputational issues that take much longer to recover from.
