# Risk Register Template

## What an integration risk register covers

A risk register captures the things that could go wrong, who owns each one, and what's being done about them. The integration risk register is distinct from the broader project risk register because integration risks have specific characteristics that don't always fit standard project risk templates.

Integration risks tend to be longer-tailed than project risks (they play out over months or years rather than weeks). They're more cross-functional (a single risk often spans IT, HR, legal, and commercial). And they're more sensitive to deal-specific factors that aren't always documented in standard project frameworks.

The template below is what I'd start from for a typical mid-market acquisition. Adapt for the specifics.

## Structure

For each risk, capture:

A short risk description, written as a specific scenario rather than a generic concern. "Identity migration runs longer than planned, blocking application consolidation and extending the integration tail" is more useful than "delivery risk."

The likelihood of the risk materialising, on whatever scale the parent organisation uses (low/medium/high or 1-5).

The impact if the risk materialises, on the same scale.

The combined risk score, derived from likelihood and impact.

The owner: the single named individual accountable for the risk.

The current mitigation: what's being done to reduce the likelihood or impact.

The contingency: what happens if the mitigation fails.

The status: open, in mitigation, monitored, accepted, or closed.

A date for the next review.

The level of detail should match the size of the deal. A small acquisition may have 10 to 15 risks tracked; a large one may have 50 or more.

## Categories of integration risk to consider

The list below isn't exhaustive but covers the categories that recur across integrations.

**Deal and commercial risks.** Material misrepresentation in the data room. Regulatory approval delays. Customer contract change-of-control issues. Working capital surprises that affect integration funding. Cyber insurance gaps during the transition.

**People and retention risks.** Key person departures, particularly in IT and engineering functions. Cultural friction reducing retention more broadly. Capacity gaps where the integration team is also the operating team. Burnout from extended integration timelines.

**Identity and access risks.** Migration timeline overruns. Privileged access exposure during the transition. Legacy admin accounts not properly retired. Conditional access carve-outs becoming permanent. Service account proliferation.

**Email and collaboration risks.** Mailbox migration data loss or corruption. Calendar disruption affecting customer-facing meetings. Shared content migration losing access permissions. Chat platform consolidation losing institutional knowledge.

**Endpoint risks.** Device re-enrolment failure rates higher than planned. Encryption coverage gaps. Patch posture regression during management platform migration. BYOD policy conflicts.

**Network and infrastructure risks.** Office connectivity transitions failing. Cloud workload migration outages. On-premise infrastructure end-of-life surprises. DNS or domain transition errors causing customer-facing impact.

**Security and compliance risks.** Inherited certifications lapsing during transition. Inherited compliance obligations not being met. Regulatory disclosure obligations from incidents discovered post-close. DLP or monitoring controls deployed without required local consultation. Cyber insurance coverage gaps.

**Application and licence risks.** Licence consolidation timing missing renewal windows. Application migrations breaking integrations with other systems. Shadow IT discovered late in the integration. Customer contracts requiring specific tooling that consolidation would breach.

**Vendor and contractual risks.** Auto-renewal clauses missed during transition. Change-of-control clauses triggering unexpected vendor responses. MSP transitions losing operational knowledge. Vendor consolidation creating concentration risk.

**Country-specific risks.** Data residency requirements not being met. Local regulatory consultation requirements being missed. Cross-border data transfer arrangements gaps. Country-specific certification requirements being inherited without resourcing.

**Cultural and process risks.** Acquired-team disengagement reducing cooperation. Decision-making patterns clashing in ways that slow the integration. Process imposition creating resentment that affects later integration phases.

## Working with the register

A few practical points on operating the risk register through the integration:

**Review weekly during the first 90 days.** The pace of new risk identification is highest in the first three months. Weekly reviews keep the register current; less frequent reviews let the register fall out of date.

**Keep the register actionable.** Risks that have been "open, monitored" for six months without movement are usually either accepted in practice (and should be marked as such) or being ignored (which means the framework isn't working).

**Make sure ownership is real.** Each risk owner should know they own the risk. Owners assigned without their knowledge are decoration, not management.

**Distinguish risks from issues.** A risk is something that might happen. An issue is something that has happened and needs handling. Mixing them in the same register reduces clarity. Some organisations use a separate issues log; some annotate risks that have crystallised. Either works as long as the distinction is maintained.

**Escalate early rather than late.** The integration team often holds risks longer than they should before escalating to the steering committee or to the deal lead. Escalating early is uncomfortable but produces better outcomes than escalating late, when the room for response has narrowed.

**Close risks formally.** Risks don't disappear; they get closed. A closed risk has either been mitigated to the point where it no longer needs active management, has materialised and been handled, or has been formally accepted by the appropriate decision-maker. Closing risks formally is what keeps the register from becoming an unmanageable list of historical concerns.
