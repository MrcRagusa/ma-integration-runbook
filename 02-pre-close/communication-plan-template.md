# Communication Plan Template

## Why communications matter for IT

Most IT integration failures I've seen aren't technical. They're communication failures dressed up as technical problems. Users surprised by changes, leadership surprised by costs, vendors surprised by contract decisions, and acquired-company employees surprised by the speed or direction of integration.

A communication plan that's been thought through during pre-close prevents most of this. It also gives the IT team a defensible record of what was communicated when, which becomes useful if the integration timeline slips and people start asking why.

## Audiences

Different audiences need different things at different points in the integration. The plan below covers the main ones.

**Acquired-company employees** need clarity on what changes immediately, what changes later, and what doesn't change at all. They also need to know where to go for help and who to escalate to if they're stuck. The first communication from parent-company IT sets the tone for the entire integration relationship; treat it accordingly.

**Acquired-company IT team** needs much more detailed information than the broader employee population, including their own role in the transition, what's expected of them in the first weeks, and the parent company's intentions about their long-term position. This conversation is sensitive, often legally constrained, and should be led jointly by HR and IT leadership.

**Acquirer-company employees**, particularly the existing IT team and helpdesk, need to know the integration is happening, what their role is, and how to handle escalations and tickets from acquired-company users.

**Senior leadership on both sides** needs regular structured updates on integration progress, costs, and risks. The format and cadence should be agreed at the start of the integration, not negotiated on the fly.

**External vendors** need contractual notifications where required, account management conversations about future direction, and timely responses to any change-of-control or assignment questions they raise.

**Customers and external parties** are usually communicated to by commercial and marketing teams rather than IT, but IT may need to support specific communications relating to system changes, email domain changes, or service availability.

## Key communication moments

**Deal announcement.** Often pre-close. Limited IT-specific content because the integration plan isn't finalised, but should signal that integration is being planned thoughtfully and that detailed information will follow. Avoid commitments that haven't been validated.

**Pre-close briefing for the acquired IT team.** Once gun-jumping rules permit, a detailed conversation with the acquired company's IT leadership about the integration plan, their role in it, and what to expect on day one. Often the most important single conversation of the integration.

**Day-one welcome.** A general communication to acquired-company employees on day one, covering what changes immediately, support channels, and a clear statement of welcome. Should not contain detailed integration timelines or technical specifics; that comes later.

**Day-one to day-30 cadence.** Regular short updates to acquired-company employees on what's happening with IT integration, what to expect in the coming weeks, and recent changes. Weekly is usually right; less frequent feels distant, more frequent feels overwhelming.

**Pre-migration notices.** Specific communications ahead of any user-affecting change, such as identity migration, mailbox migration, endpoint re-enrolment, or application consolidation. These need lead time, clear instructions, and a named contact for support.

**Milestone announcements.** Communications when major integration milestones are completed, used both to acknowledge the work and to maintain visibility of progress.

**Issue communications.** Honest, prompt communications when something has gone wrong. Pretending issues haven't happened damages trust permanently; acknowledging them and explaining the response builds it.

## Tone and content principles

Lead with the user impact, not the technical detail. Most users don't care which identity provider they're moving onto; they care whether their email still works on Tuesday.

Be honest about what you don't yet know. Saying "we're still working out the timeline for X and we'll update you by Y" is better than guessing or staying silent.

Avoid corporate language imported from the acquirer's culture without checking how it lands at the acquired company. What sounds neutral in one company can sound bureaucratic or cold in another, particularly across cultures and languages. For acquisitions in non-English-speaking markets, get senior local-language speakers to review communications before they go out.

Keep the IT integration team identifiable. Users should know there are real people running the work and how to reach them. Generic "IT" sender addresses without named owners reduce the perceived quality of the integration.

## A simple plan structure

For each major communication, capture: audience, sender, channel, content summary, timing, and approval owner. A simple table is fine. The point is having a single shared document that the integration team, HR, and communications can work from, not creating a polished deliverable.

Review the plan weekly during pre-close and the first 30 days post-close. Communication needs change as the integration progresses, and a plan that isn't reviewed becomes a plan that's quietly out of date.
