# Vendor Consolidation

## What "vendor consolidation" actually delivers

Vendor consolidation gets discussed mostly in terms of cost. The savings story is the easy one to tell: combining seat counts at scale unlocks better per-unit pricing, and reducing the total number of vendors removes overhead from procurement, finance, and IT.

The savings are real and worth pursuing. But in my experience, the more durable benefit of vendor consolidation isn't the price reduction. It's that fewer vendor relationships translate into fewer integration points, fewer support escalations, fewer contract renewals to manage, and fewer points of failure. A consolidated vendor estate is operationally simpler, and operational simplicity compounds over time in a way that one-off cost savings don't.

That framing matters because it changes the consolidation decisions you make. If the only goal is cost, you optimise for the cheapest combination. If the goal is also operational simplicity, you sometimes accept slightly higher unit cost in exchange for vendors who are easier to work with, more strategic to the business, or more durable in their commercial position.

## The categories of vendor that matter most

Across integrations, certain vendor categories generate disproportionate consolidation value:

**Microsoft, Google, or equivalent platform vendors.** These are usually the largest single vendor relationships in the IT estate, and the consolidation opportunities at renewal are significant. Combining acquired-company users into the parent's enterprise agreement typically unlocks per-user discounts that scale with the combined seat count. The work is mostly contractual rather than technical, but the timing has to align with the renewal cycle to capture full value.

**Cloud providers.** AWS, Azure, GCP, and similar. Consolidation onto the parent's accounts usually unlocks committed-use discounts that weren't available at the acquired company's smaller scale. Beyond cost, cloud consolidation simplifies governance, security monitoring, and cost allocation in ways that pay back beyond the immediate savings.

**Security tooling vendors.** Endpoint protection, email security, SIEM, identity providers, and DLP. Consolidation here delivers both cost savings and operational simplification, since the security operations team manages a single stack rather than multiple parallel ones.

**Managed service providers.** The acquired company's MSPs often duplicate services the parent already has internally, or duplicate services from MSPs the parent already retains. The decision is whether to absorb the work in-house, transfer it to the parent's existing MSP, or maintain the acquired MSP for specific services. Each decision has different cost, capacity, and continuity implications.

**Print, hardware, and lifecycle management vendors.** Often overlooked because the contracts are smaller, but the consolidation opportunities are real and the operational simplification is meaningful. In one integration I worked on at BW, renegotiating the managed print contract specifically generated material annual savings as part of a broader consolidation programme.

**Telephony and contact centre vendors.** Voice services, contact centre platforms, conferencing services. Often involve specialist contracts with longer terms and more complex commercial structures than typical SaaS, but the consolidation can be valuable where both companies have substantial voice estates.

**Insurance, particularly cyber insurance.** Acquired companies sometimes have separate cyber insurance arrangements that overlap with the parent's group policy. Consolidation reduces premium and aligns coverage, but the timing and specific policy terms need careful review.

## The renewal-driven approach

The most reliable way to capture vendor consolidation value is to drive it through contract renewal cycles rather than through forced mid-term renegotiation.

Mid-term renegotiation works for some vendors, particularly those with strong account management relationships and visible interest in expansion at the parent. It works less well for vendors who view the deal as already concluded and have no commercial reason to revisit terms.

Renewal-driven consolidation puts the integration team in a stronger position. The vendor knows the renewal is contestable, that consolidation alternatives exist, and that the combined entity has more leverage than either company had separately. The renewal becomes a structured negotiation rather than a one-sided ask.

The mechanics: maintain a forward-looking pipeline of vendor renewals, with at least 12 months of visibility. For each renewal, identify the consolidation question (combine with parent contract, replace with parent vendor, retain at acquired terms, or other) and the negotiating leverage. Brief the procurement and account-management leads in advance. Don't allow the renewal to happen by default and then look at consolidation options afterwards.

## Negotiating leverage

Integration creates negotiating leverage that the original companies didn't have separately. Using it well requires being explicit about it.

Combined seat count and combined spend matter to vendor pricing. A combined entity at 1,500 seats is a different commercial proposition from two separate entities at 500 and 1,000. Most vendors have pricing tiers that aren't visible until you actually negotiate at the new scale.

Multi-year commitments unlock additional discounts but reduce future flexibility. The right answer depends on how confident the parent is that the application or service is durable in its strategy. For platforms central to the long-term architecture, multi-year commitments are usually worth it. For tools that may be replaced, single-year terms preserve optionality.

The credible threat of moving to an alternative vendor is the strongest commercial leverage. It's only credible if the alternative is genuinely viable. Negotiating against an alternative the parent isn't actually willing to use is theatre that vendors recognise.

Account team relationships matter more than people expect. A vendor's account team usually has discretion within commercial parameters, and they advocate internally for the deals they understand and believe in. Making the integration story clear, the consolidation logic compelling, and the customer relationship valuable to the vendor's business gets better outcomes than treating the negotiation as purely transactional.

## Sequencing decisions

Not all vendor consolidations should happen at the same time. A few sequencing principles:

Consolidate the largest vendors first. The savings concentrate at the top of the spend distribution, and the work to consolidate one large vendor is usually similar to the work to consolidate several smaller ones.

Consolidate the easiest decisions before the hardest ones. Building a track record of successful consolidations gives the integration team credibility for harder conversations later.

Don't try to consolidate during periods when the vendor relationship is already under stress. If a vendor is in the middle of a major incident, a contractual dispute, or a substantial implementation, postpone the consolidation conversation until the relationship is on stable ground.

Allow time between consolidations of related services. Migrating from one endpoint protection vendor to another, then immediately starting a similar migration with email security, exhausts the team and the user base. Space the work out across quarters where possible.

## What good looks like at 18 months

Eighteen months after a mid-market acquisition, an integration that has handled vendor consolidation well will have:

A clearly smaller vendor list than the two original companies' combined estate. The reduction is visible in the procurement system, not just in the IT team's view.

Renegotiated terms on the largest vendor contracts, capturing the scale benefits of the combined entity.

Made deliberate decisions on every major vendor relationship, with the decisions documented and revisited at the appropriate review cadence.

Captured cumulative vendor savings in a form that finance can attribute to the integration, with the savings showing up in the operating budget rather than as one-off line items.

Eighteen months in, vendor consolidation should feel less like an integration activity and more like a normal operating discipline. If it still feels like an integration project, the work hasn't yet been absorbed into how the consolidated entity runs.
