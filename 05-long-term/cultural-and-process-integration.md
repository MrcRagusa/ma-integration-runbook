# Cultural and Process Integration

## Why an IT runbook covers this

Cultural and process integration sounds like an HR or organisational development concern, not an IT one. The reason it appears in an IT integration runbook is that IT teams sit at the intersection of every other function, and the cultural dynamics of the integration play out through IT as much as anywhere else.

How IT decisions get made, how change gets communicated, how problems get escalated, and how shared services get governed all reflect the underlying cultural patterns of the merging organisations. Getting the cultural and process integration right inside IT is one of the strongest signals to the rest of the business about whether the broader integration is working.

This file is shorter and more reflective than the operational ones in earlier folders. The work here is genuinely less structured than identity migration or licence consolidation, and pretending otherwise would be misleading.

## The patterns that recur

Across the integrations I've worked on, a few patterns surface consistently in the IT relationship between acquirer and acquired.

**The acquired team's expertise is undervalued.** The default assumption is that the parent's way of doing things is correct and the acquired team needs to adopt it. Sometimes this is right; often it isn't. The acquired team has solved specific problems in specific ways for reasons that aren't always immediately visible, and overriding their patterns without understanding them creates regression rather than progress.

**The parent's pace feels slow.** Acquired teams, particularly from smaller companies, often experience the parent's change cadence as bureaucratic and slow. What feels like discipline from inside the parent feels like inertia from outside. The reverse can also be true, where the parent feels the acquired team is rushing decisions that need broader consultation.

**Tooling becomes proxy for autonomy.** Disagreements about which project management tool, which chat platform, or which document standard wins are rarely really about the tools. They're about whether the acquired team's existing way of working is being respected or being replaced. Being aware of this lets you handle the conversations on the right terrain.

**The integration team becomes the face of the parent.** Acquired-company users form their view of the parent organisation largely through their interactions with the integration team. If those interactions are respectful, transparent, and competent, the broader integration goes more smoothly. If they're imposed, opaque, or dismissive, the friction shows up everywhere else.

**Communication patterns reveal cultural differences.** Some organisations communicate through structured documents, some through chat threads, some through meetings. Some treat written commitments as binding, others as conversational. These differences are easy to miss until they cause specific operational issues, and they're usually cultural rather than technical.

## What works in practice

The patterns I've found that improve the cultural integration of IT specifically:

**Build a joint integration team where possible.** Where the acquired company has a competent IT function, integrating that team into the integration leadership rather than treating them as recipients of the integration produces better outcomes. Joint decision-making creates joint ownership.

**Be explicit about what's being preserved.** Clear statements about what aspects of the acquired company's operating model will be retained reduce anxiety. Vague statements about "the best of both" are heard as code for "the parent's way."

**Resist the urge to immediately standardise low-stakes things.** Naming conventions, ticket categorisations, status update formats, meeting cadences. Each individually is small. Imposing all of them simultaneously communicates that nothing about the acquired company's way of working will be allowed to continue.

**Treat the acquired company's IT lead as a peer.** Specifically: include them in parent-company IT leadership conversations, give them voice in decisions affecting their estate, and provide them with information rather than instructions wherever the situation allows. The acquired IT lead is usually the single most important relationship in the IT integration.

**Document decisions and the reasons for them.** Cultural integration is undermined by decisions that appear arbitrary. The same decision, with the reasoning explained, lands differently. Most of this documentation is for the acquired team's benefit; some of it is for the parent's team, who also need to understand why decisions were made the way they were.

**Make change cadence visible.** Rather than letting changes arrive unpredictably, publish a change calendar that the acquired team can see. Predictability reduces friction even when the changes themselves aren't welcomed.

## Process integration specifically

Beyond the cultural dynamics, there's a body of process work that needs to happen between IT functions during integration.

Service desk processes need to converge. Different ticketing platforms, different categorisations, different SLAs, different escalation paths. The convergence work is mechanical but the operational impact is real, and the speed of convergence affects how the user base experiences the integration.

Change management processes need to align. Acquired companies with mature change processes don't always tolerate having those processes overridden by parent-company processes that are different but not obviously better. Acquired companies without mature change processes may find the parent's processes restrictive. Both situations need direct conversation, not implicit imposition.

Vendor management and procurement processes need to align. The acquired company's existing procurement patterns persist long after the formal integration unless they're explicitly replaced. Bringing acquired-company purchases through the parent's procurement processes is administratively dull but commercially significant.

Information security processes need to extend. Incident response, vulnerability management, access reviews, and the wider operating rhythm of the security function need to extend to cover the acquired environment. Not by gradually drifting, but by deliberate extension.

Reporting and metrics need to consolidate. The acquired company often had its own IT metrics and its own reporting cadences. Bringing the consolidated entity onto a single set of metrics is straightforward technically and surprisingly difficult organisationally, because metrics are usually entangled with how performance gets discussed and rewarded.

## When integration is "complete"

There's no clean point at which cultural and process integration is finished. Useful indicators that the work has matured to a sustainable place:

The acquired team and the parent's team operate as a single organisation, with shared metrics, shared rhythms, and shared decision-making, rather than as two teams in a transitional arrangement.

People from the acquired company are visible in roles that aren't only about the acquired estate. Promotion, lateral moves, and new responsibilities flow in both directions, not just from parent into acquired.

Decisions about future direction are made jointly, not on behalf of the acquired team. The "us" and "them" framing has receded.

The next acquisition, when it happens, finds an IT function that's better at integration than it was last time, because the lessons from the previous integration were captured and applied.

That last point matters. In acquisitive organisations, integration capability itself is a compounding asset. Each integration that's handled well builds the capability for the next one. Each integration that's handled poorly leaves gaps that have to be filled before the next one can start. The cultural and process integration work is what determines which of those two patterns you're on.
